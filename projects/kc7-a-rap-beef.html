Scenario and scope

What I was investigating

In KC7's fictional OWL Records scenario, a public feud between artists Dwake and Present creates a possible security exposure. Acting as an OWL Records security analyst, I examined whether someone used public information about Dwake to target his account.

Fictional training investigation

This walkthrough documents the evidence captured during my KC7 replay. Some queries were supplied by the guided exercise; reconstructed or extended queries are identified below. Scenario narrative, observed logs and analyst interpretation are distinguished throughout.

Initial lead

Establish the organisation and narrow the web activity

I first used the Employees table to locate the CEO record, then investigated the supplied source IP in InboundNetworkEvents. The employee lookup established the organisation's dataset and introduced fields such as usernames and email addresses; it did not identify the web visitor.

Employees
| where role == "CEO"

The query returned Sean Crater as CEO. For the network lead, I applied the exercise's time window and source IP:

InboundNetworkEvents
| where timestamp between (datetime("2024-04-10T00:00:00") .. datetime("2024-04-11T00:00:00"))
| where src_ip == "18.66.52.227"

This yielded 19 web events. I reviewed the timestamps, requested URLs and status codes to understand what the visitor sought. The IP is a scenario indicator, not proof of a person's identity.

Evidence pivots

Contact searches led to an account recovery route

01
Search intent

Requested URLs repeatedly searched for Dwake's email address, contact details and booking information. This pattern pointed to targeted information gathering.

02
Account identifier

The activity exposed dwake_audrey@owl-records.com and showed a password reset request associated with username dwaubrey. Later authentication and mailbox events use dwaudrey; I preserve each identifier as recorded rather than silently treating the spellings as identical.

03
Recovery questions

Subsequent requests referenced a mother's maiden name and a first pet. The investigation then compared those prompts with details disclosed in Dwake's verse.

Account recovery activity

Correlating public clues with the submitted answers

The verse disclosed the values Washington and Fluffy. A web event from 18.66.52.227 at 2024-04-10 13:02:47 UTC contained those values in a request to the OWL Records security-question endpoint. This ties the public clues to a concrete recovery request.

InboundNetworkEvents
| where src_ip == "18.66.52.227"
| where url has "Washington" and url has "Fluffy"
| project timestamp, method, src_ip, url, status_code

The query above is a compact reconstruction of the filtering approach for this write-up; the KC7 exercise supplied some of the tutorial queries. The logged request used the two answers as URL parameters and returned HTTP 200. That response shows the endpoint handled the request; by itself it does not establish that the answers were accepted or the password was changed.

Correlating DNS and email

Identify the phishing infrastructure

I pivoted from the known IP to PassiveDns, which associated 18.66.52.227 with betterlyrics4u[.]com.

PassiveDns
| where ip == "18.66.52.227"

The tutorial query Email | take 10 helped inspect the email schema and identify the link column. Filtering that column found emails containing the domain:

Email
| where link has "betterlyrics4u.com"

The exercise confirmed that ghostwritersanonymous@protonmail.com sent most matching emails. wemakebeatz@gmail.com also appeared in the results, but its presence alone does not establish the same operator. A total email count was not captured.

To understand targeting, the guided query collected unique recipients and matched them against employee records:

let _targets = Email
| where link has "betterlyrics4u.com"
| distinct recipient;
Employees
| where email_addr in (_targets)

The most targeted role was Rapper, supporting an artist-focused campaign. Dwake received an email at 2024-04-15 11:07:12 UTC from ghostwritersanonymous@protonmail.com, with the same reply-to address, addressed to dwake_audrey@owl-records.com.

[EXTERNAL] RE: Need a ghostwriter for your next hit?

The link was hxxp://betterlyrics4u[.]com/share/online/published/enter. The email verdict was CLEAN. The ghostwriting lure fits the recipients' roles; the RE: prefix suggests a reply but does not prove an earlier conversation. The verdict did not resolve the email's safety.

Link access and authentication

Connect the email to a successful attacker login

I searched outbound events for the exact email URL and Dwake's scenario IP, 10.10.0.5.

OutboundNetworkEvents
| where url == "http://betterlyrics4u.com/share/online/published/enter"
| where src_ip == "10.10.0.5"

A GET request occurred at 2024-04-15T12:03:12Z, 56 minutes after the email timestamp. This shows access to the URL; it does not by itself show credential submission.

AuthenticationEvents
| where username == "dwaudrey"
| where src_ip == "18.66.52.227"

The authentication query returned one record: Successful Login on MAIL-SERVER01 at 2024-04-15T13:03:12Z, exactly one hour after link access. A successful login from the known adversary IP supports account compromise. The exercise states that the adversaries obtained the credentials; the captured screenshots do not show the submission itself.

The earlier scenario narrative also confirms an email takeover followed by an Instagram password reset and an embarrassing post. That is narrative context for the earlier recovery episode, not an outcome inferred solely from its HTTP 200 response.

Activity after compromise

Trace mailbox searches and the archive download

The guided investigation returned to inbound web logs to inspect activity against Dwake's mailbox.

InboundNetworkEvents
| where timestamp between (datetime("2024-04-12T00:00:00") .. datetime("2024-05-01T00:00:00"))
| where url has "dwaudrey"
| where src_ip has "18.66.52.227"

This returned 10 records. The exercise describes the activity as searches for sensitive information; the visible rows include requests on 16 April. The final selected event, at 2024-04-30T14:23:02Z, was a GET request to /mail/readmail from the adversary IP, with mailbox_folder=Drafts and download=true&output=DwakesDirtySecrets.zip. It returned HTTP 200.

The exercise confirms DwakesDirtySecrets.zip as the archive used to steal information. The log supports a successful download response, but the captured evidence does not reveal the archive contents, transfer size or subsequent destination.

Analyst reflection

What this investigation taught me

  • Pivot between evidence sources: an IP led to a domain, the domain to emails, recipients to roles, and the email URL to web and authentication activity.
  • Separate delivery, access and compromise: an email record, a GET request and a successful login support different conclusions.
  • Build a defensible timeline: correlating UTC timestamps linked the lure to later account activity without assuming unseen actions.
  • Assess recovery design: publicly discoverable answers are weak recovery factors, and including them in GET URLs exposes them in telemetry.
  • Scope the impact: artist-focused targeting and a mailbox archive download expand the incident beyond one suspicious email.

In a real response, I would investigate other recipients, revoke affected sessions, reset compromised credentials, review recovery settings and MFA, block confirmed malicious infrastructure, and examine mailbox access and download records. These are proposed response actions, not actions performed in the lab.

This was a guided training exercise, not a production incident. The captured evidence supports the documented login and download activity; it does not establish the attacker’s real identity or the exact stolen content.
